Frequently Asked Questions

Answers to the questions business owners and families ask most about financial planning, insurance, and estate planning in Singapore.

Key person insurance is a life or disability policy taken out by a business on its most critical individuals — founders, directors, top salespeople, or anyone whose absence would cause significant financial harm.

In Singapore, losing a key person can mean lost revenue, broken client relationships, difficulty securing financing, and operational disruption. Key person insurance provides a lump sum that covers recruitment costs, revenue shortfalls, and debt obligations while the business stabilises.

It's especially important for SMEs where a single individual often drives a disproportionate share of business value. As a specialist in corporate insurance advisory, I help businesses identify their key person risks and design appropriate coverage strategies.

Estate planning in Singapore starts with understanding what happens to your assets without a plan. Under the Intestate Succession Act, assets are distributed according to a fixed formula that rarely reflects your actual wishes.

The first step is to draft a legally valid will. Beyond that, consider setting up trusts for complex asset structures, creating a Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA) in case of mental incapacity, and reviewing your CPF nominations and insurance beneficiary designations.

For business owners, estate planning should also address business succession — who takes over, how it's funded, and how to minimise disruption. Working with a qualified estate planning advisor like an Advanced Estate Planning Practitioner (AEPP) ensures all elements are coordinated into a comprehensive plan.

Singapore offers several financial planning tools specifically for families with special needs dependants:

Special Needs Trust Company (SNTC) — Singapore's only non-profit trust company dedicated to persons with special needs. SNTC manages trusts that ensure funds are used for your loved one's care even after you pass on.

CPF Special Needs Savings Scheme (SNSS) — This allows CPF members to nominate their savings to provide regular monthly payouts to a special needs beneficiary, rather than a lump sum that may be difficult to manage.

Insurance Planning — Life insurance policies can be structured to fund an SNTC trust upon the policyholder's death, creating a reliable source of long-term care funding.

A comprehensive plan typically combines all three elements along with a detailed Letter of Intent outlining the dependant's care needs, routines, and preferences.

Business owners face unique financial complexities that personal financial planning alone doesn't address. Your personal wealth is often tied directly to your business, creating concentration risk. You need to balance reinvesting in growth with building personal financial security.

Issues like key person risk, business succession, buy-sell agreements, group insurance for employees, and estate planning for business assets all require specialist knowledge that goes beyond typical personal finance.

A financial advisor who understands both personal and corporate financial planning can create an integrated strategy that protects the business, optimises your personal wealth, and ensures a smooth transition when the time comes — whether that's retirement, sale, or succession.

The Million Dollar Round Table (MDRT) is a global, independent association of the world's leading life insurance and financial services professionals. MDRT membership is earned annually based on strict ethical and production criteria — it's not a title you can buy or be appointed.

Achieving MDRT status demonstrates that an advisor has a proven track record of serving a significant number of clients at a high standard, maintains ethical practices, and is committed to ongoing professional development.

When choosing a financial advisor in Singapore, MDRT membership is one important indicator — alongside qualifications like CFP (Certified Financial Planner) and AEPP (Advanced Estate Planning Practitioner) — that the advisor is experienced, competent, and respected by their peers.

The cost of financial advisory in Singapore varies depending on the type of service. Many financial advisors, including insurance-based advisors, are compensated through commissions on the products they recommend, meaning there is no direct fee to the client for the advisory itself.

Fee-based advisors may charge a flat fee, hourly rate, or percentage of assets under management. The structures vary widely across the industry.

For initial consultations, many advisors — including myself — offer complimentary, no-obligation sessions to understand your needs before recommending any solutions. The key is to ensure your advisor is transparent about how they are compensated and that their recommendations genuinely serve your best interests.
